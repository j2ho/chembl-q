ChEMBL‑Q

A leakage-resistant virtual screening benchmark, curated from ChEMBL 36

Most released virtual screening models are trained on PDBbind or BioLiP and then scored on DUD-E, DEKOIS 2.0 or LIT-PCBA. Those benchmarks were built from targets that have crystal structures, which is the criterion that put the same targets in PDBbind and BioLiP, so the models are being scored on receptors they were trained on. ChEMBL-Q keeps the two apart. 1,006 targets are offered for training and they are the ones those sets already cover; 407 are held back as the benchmark, and none of them has a homologue in PDBbind, in BioLiP, or in the training split itself. Whatever a model was trained on, it can be scored here.

the problem

A PDBbind-trained model has already seen the benchmarks it is scored on

DUD-E, DEKOIS 2.0 and LIT-PCBA were assembled from targets that have crystal structures, which is the same criterion that put those targets in PDBbind and BioLiP. So the sets overlap, and the overlap is close to total. We measured it with the rules and the superposition described below, applying them to all four benchmarks through one code path.

Every DUD-E target has a pocket within 1 Å of something in PDBbind or BioLiP, and for more than half the distance is 0.00 Å — the same deposited structure. Every DEKOIS 2.0 and LIT-PCBA target has a sequence homologue there. Scoring a structure-trained model on any of the three measures how well it recalls its training receptors, mixed in with whatever ranking ability it has.

ChEMBL-Q's 0% is not a discovery: holding those targets out is the rule, and the next section states it. The pocket column is the informative one, because nothing in the split is decided on geometry. Pocket overlap falls from 100% to 14.6% as a consequence of separating on sequence and structure identity, not because any pocket was filtered.

LIT-PCBA ships several structures per target; a target counts as overlapping if its closest structure does. Pocket figures cover the 369 ChEMBL-Q and 14 LIT-PCBA targets whose representative structure yields a pocket of at least 6 residues at an 8 Å radius.

how the split is made

Three rules, none of them geometric

A target is kept out of the test set if any of the following holds. All three are sequence or identifier rules; pocket geometry is never used to decide a split.

  1. Sequence homology. An entry in PDBbind or BioLiP aligns to it at 0.4 identity or better over at least 80% of that external sequence. Coverage is measured on the external side because what gets crystallised is a domain while a ChEMBL target is a whole UniProt entry. 978 targets
  2. Shared structure. It was built from a PDB entry those sets already hold. Alignment cannot be relied on here: MMseqs2 finds only a 31-residue alignment between IGF2R's 2,491 residues and its 182-residue PDBbind construct, so four targets kept their own structure in the test set until this rule was added. 680 targets
  3. Proximity to train. After clustering, any test target still within 0.4 identity of a train sequence is demoted, iterated to a fixed point. Coverage counts over whichever of the two sequences the alignment spans, so a short training sequence sitting whole inside a long target still counts. 3 targets

1,074 targets in total. Only then, as a check on work already done, comes the pocket measurement: for every target, the distance to its closest PDBbind or BioLiP pocket. If the rules above did their job the two splits should separate here too, and they do.

positive control

A measurement like this is worth nothing without a control. 305 targets have, as their closest external pocket, the very same PDB entry they were built from. All 305 superpose at RMSD ≤ 0.163 Å, median exactly 0.000. We evaluated FoldDisco as a faster route and rejected it on this same test: 79% of its self-comparisons failed to return zero. The exhaustive sweep runs in 72 minutes on 32 cores, so no shortcut was needed.

the residue

The overlap that remains is between different proteins

1,074 targets were held out of the test set: 978 for direct sequence homology to PDBbind or BioLiP, 680 because they were built from a PDB entry those sets already hold, and 3 for proximity to a training sequence. What survives that is a different question. Of the 369 test targets with a measurable external match, 165 still sit within 2 Å of some external pocket. Not one of them is the same protein:

None of the 165 shares a PDB entry with PDBbind or BioLiP — that is now enforced, not observed. Beyond that they are simply not close relatives: 104 of them produce no sequence alignment with the matched entry at all, and among the 62 that do the median identity is 0.30 and the highest is 0.51. These are conserved sites in families that have diverged — nucleotide and cofactor pockets, ADP, ATP-γ-S, GDP, coenzyme A, and the aminergic receptor site below. Whether a model that has seen one of those has thereby seen an unrelated protein carrying the same site is a real question, and not one a curation pipeline should answer by deleting the evidence.

Getting to zero took more than sequence search. Four test targets had their own representative PDB entry sitting in the external sets even after the coverage rule was corrected, because MMseqs2 reported only a 31-residue alignment between IGF2R's 2,491 residues and its 182-residue PDBbind construct. The PDB identifier settles what the alignment cannot, so any target built from an entry those sets hold is now blocked outright.

We could have cut on pocket distance too. We measured what that would cost first.

A 1.0 Å cut removes 13% of the test targets but 22% of the actives, and a 2.0 Å cut removes 41% of the targets and 56% of the actives. The loss always runs ahead of the count, and that is not a coincidence: a fold that has been drugged hard is also one PDBbind and BioLiP hold many structures of, so “pocket already seen” and “has enough compounds to benchmark on” select on the same underlying property.

So the number ships rather than the knife. Every row of chembl_targets.tsv carries ext_pocket_rmsd and the entry it matched. Score a model across the whole test set, or restrict to a pocket-novel subset at whatever threshold your claim needs, without rebuilding anything.

LIT-PCBA is the interesting row. Every one of its targets is in PDBbind or BioLiP, yet the compounds crystallised with those proteins look nothing like the actives it asks a model to find: median Tanimoto 0.159, and one active in a thousand has a neighbour at 0.7. Protein-level containment and compound-level novelty are separable, and LIT-PCBA has both at once. DUD-E and DEKOIS do not: even restricted to the same protein, about one active in twelve has a near-duplicate already sitting in a crystal structure, and loosening the identity cut to 0.4 only raises that.

ChEMBL-Q's dash is the split rule showing through rather than a measurement: no test target has a homologous external entry, so there are no ligands to gather. It says nothing about how novel the compounds are, which is what the next section measures and where this dataset is weakest.

Compounds, not just receptors

Everything above is about the protein. The compounds leak too, and by a different route. ChEMBL-Q splits on targets, so a compound active against a test target may also be active against a train target and appear on both sides. That is not a defect of the split so much as a property of splitting on targets at all, but it is real and it is the largest overlap anywhere in this dataset.

Against the crystallised ligands of PDBbind and BioLiP the three benchmarks sit close together, and LIT-PCBA is the cleanest of them. Against its own training actives ChEMBL-Q is not clean at all: 14.2% of test compounds are also train compounds, 29.7% share a Murcko scaffold with one, and 15.0% have a training neighbour at Tanimoto 0.9 or above. DUD-E and LIT-PCBA have no comparable number because neither defines a training split.

A model can exploit that without looking at a receptor at all. If you need a compound-disjoint evaluation, build it from the shipped per-target actives.tsv files rather than assuming the target split gave you one; the ligand-only control belongs in any benchmark report using this dataset. Removing the overlap here instead was not obviously right — it would delete a compound from the target it was measured against because of a measurement on a different target.

known limitation

The test set is small, and half its targets carry only a couple of actives. That is the honest cost of holding out everything PDBbind and BioLiP have seen: 1,074 targets are held out of it, and the well-studied ones are exactly the ones those sets cover. 93 test targets have 10 or more actives. Pick a floor that suits your evaluation, and say which floor you picked.

how it is built

Eight stages

Each stage is a separate command reading the previous stage's output, so a run can be resumed or a single stage re-run with different settings. The numbering is a real dependency order — the pocket leakage check writes a table the split reads.

pipeline
#commanddoestime
1curatePulls actives, measured inactives and conflicts out of ChEMBL. Labels by relation, not by threshold alone: a censored “>” record is non-binding evidence and its strength is the concentration tested.~30 m
2filter-proteinsFetches structures (mmCIF fallback, shared cache), aligns them, keeps targets with a single pocket, picks one representative ligand by contacting residues.~3 h
3cluster-activesButina clustering per target.~1 m
4build-poolGlobal compound pool with properties and fingerprints.~1 m
5receptor-simSequence identity, then order-free pocket RMSD over 989,121 internal pairs.~6 m
6select-decoysProperty-matched decoys, excluding anything measured against this target or active against a similar receptor.~45 m
7external-pockets
pocket-leakage		Extracts 38,825 PDBbind and BioLiP pockets, then superposes every ChEMBL pocket against all of them.~75 m
8splitClusters at 0.4 identity, blocks any target with an external homologue, separates test from train to a fixed point.~2 m

reproduce it

Run it yourself

Needs Python 3.10+, MMseqs2 on PATH, and the ChEMBL SQLite release. config.json is the scientific contract — running without it falls back to much looser defaults and will not reproduce this dataset. 

# install
git clone https://github.com/j2ho/chembl-q
cd chembl-q
pip install -e .

# the whole pipeline, at the settings this dataset was built with
bash run_full.sh

# or one stage at a time
chembl-curator curate --database chembl_36.db --config config.json --output data/
chembl-curator filter-proteins --curated-dir data/ --n-processes 32 --cache-dir pdb_cache/
chembl-curator cluster-actives --data-dir data/ --dist-thresh 0.3
chembl-curator build-pool --data-dir data/
chembl-curator receptor-sim --data-dir data/ --mode pocket --pocket-method hungarian --pocket-radius 8.0
chembl-curator select-decoys --data-dir data/ --pocket-rmsd-thresh 2.0 --min-matched-residues 15
chembl-curator pocket-leakage --data-dir data/ --cache external_pockets.npz
chembl-curator split --data-dir data/ --seqid 0.4 --valid-frac 1.0
stage 7 without the raw sets

Extracting external pockets needs PDBbind and BioLiP structures on disk, which most people will not have. The repository ships external_pockets.npz — all 38,825 pockets, 17 MB — so pocket-leakage runs without them. Skip both and the split simply omits its two extra columns.
